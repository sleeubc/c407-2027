Answer: make the table wide, then divide

wide <- yearly |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = act_use_cat,
              values_from = avg_price)
wide
# A tibble: 4 × 3
  sale_year `S/F Res` `Strata Res`
      <dbl>     <dbl>        <dbl>
1      2022   2000000       800000
2      2023   1900000       800000
3      2024   1850000       780000
4      2025   1800000       800000
wide |>
  mutate(ratio = `S/F Res` / `Strata Res`)
# A tibble: 4 × 4
  sale_year `S/F Res` `Strata Res` ratio
      <dbl>     <dbl>        <dbl> <dbl>
1      2022   2000000       800000  2.5 
2      2023   1900000       800000  2.38
3      2024   1850000       780000  2.37
4      2025   1800000       800000  2.25
  • names_from: the column whose values become the new column names. values_from: the column that fills them. Every other column, here sale_year, decides the rows.
  • Each type is now a column, so the ratio is one mutate(). S/F Res is not a valid R name, so it needs backticks; without them, R reads S/F as S divided by F.

Quick check: what is wrong with this chart?

renters_w <- tibble(city = c("Vancouver", "Surrey"),
                    `2001` = c(56, 26), `2006` = c(52, 25), `2016` = c(53, 28), `2021` = c(55, 31))
renters <- renters_w |>
  pivot_longer(cols = -city, names_to = "year",
               values_to = "renter_pct")
ggplot(renters, aes(year, renter_pct,
                    colour = city)) +
  geom_point()

The years are evenly spaced, so 2006 sits as close to 2016 as to 2001. names_to makes year text, and ggplot spaces text as categories. Add mutate(year = as.numeric(year)) after the pivot.

  • The renter shares are made up. 2011 is missing on purpose: the 2011 long-form census was the voluntary National Household Survey, which analysts often leave out.
  • With geom_line() instead of geom_point(), the chart is empty, with the message “Each group consists of only one observation”: text on the x-axis makes each year its own group.

Practice: the single-family premium

bca <- read_rds("data/bca262.rds")

Using bca, for the City of Vancouver from 2016 to 2025:

  1. compute the median price of each act_use_cat in each year;
  2. make the table wide, with one column per type;
  3. add the ratio of the single-family median to the strata median.

In which year was the ratio highest?

Answer

premium <- bca |>
  filter(juris_label == "City of Vancouver", year(sale_date) %in% 2016:2025) |>
  group_by(act_use_cat, sale_year = year(sale_date)) |>
  summarise(median_price = median(price, na.rm = TRUE), .groups = "drop") |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = act_use_cat, values_from = median_price) |>
  mutate(ratio = `S/F Res` / `Strata Res`) |>
  arrange(desc(ratio))
premium |> head(4)
# A tibble: 4 × 4
  sale_year `S/F Res` `Strata Res` ratio
      <dbl>     <dbl>        <dbl> <dbl>
1      2016   2110000       560000  3.77
2      2017   1935000       655000  2.95
3      2018   1864000       680000  2.74
4      2022   2220000       830000  2.67

In 2016, a single-family home cost 3.8 times a strata home. In 2025, the ratio was 2.4.

Which rows does each join keep?

Code Keeps
left_join(x, y) Every row of x, with NA where y has no match
inner_join(x, y) Only the rows of x with a match in y
full_join(x, y) Every row of both tables
right_join(x, y) Every row of y

Each join adds the columns of y to x. They differ only in which rows they keep.

  • sales |> full_join(area_info, by = "neigh") gives five rows: the four sales and West End, with NA for sale_id and price.
  • right_join(x, y) is left_join(y, x) with the columns in a different order. It is rare in practice.

Keys can have different names

stations <- tibble(
  station = c("Oakridge - 41st Avenue", "Langara - 49th Avenue", "Marine Drive"),
  geo_local_area = c("Oakridge", "Oakridge", "Marpole")

The City of Vancouver’s station data call the local area geo_local_area. Name both columns with join_by(), the left table’s first:

sales |>

A duplicate key adds rows

nrow(sales)
[1] 4
sales |>
  left_join(stations,
            by = join_by(neigh == geo_local_area)) |>
  nrow()
[1] 5

Check the key before you join:

stations |>
  count(geo_local_area) |>
  filter(n > 1)
# A tibble: 1 × 2
  geo_local_area     n
  <chr>          <int>
1 Oakridge           2

Oakridge has two stations, so its sale matched twice. Any average or count over the joined rows now counts it twice.

relationship = makes R check for you

sales |>
  left_join(stations, by = join_by(neigh == geo_local_area),
            relationship = "many-to-one")
Error in `left_join()`:
! Each row in `x` must match at most 1 row in `y`.
ℹ Row 2 of `x` matches multiple rows in `y`.

"many-to-one": many sales may share a key, but each key may appear only once in stations. The silent duplicate becomes an error.

  • Without relationship =, dplyr warns only when keys repeat in both tables (“many-to-many”). A key that repeats in one table adds rows silently.
  • "one-to-one" also requires each key to appear only once in the left table.

Predict the output

How many rows, and which sales?

anti_join() finds the rows that did not match

sales |>
  anti_join(area_info, by = "neigh")
# A tibble: 1 × 3
  sale_id neigh               price
    <int> <chr>               <dbl>
1       4 Dunbar-Southlands 2100000

anti_join(x, y) returns the rows of x with no match in y, and only the columns of x. Run it before an inner_join() to see what you will drop, and after a left_join() to see which rows got NA.

After every join, check both directions: rows that matched twice (relationship =) and rows that did not match (anti_join()). A row count alone can miss them when they cancel out.

  • Deck 08 uses anti_join() on real data: most of BC Assessment’s neighbourhood names have no City local area of the same name.

Application: repeat sales

A repeat sale compares a home with itself

van <- bca |> filter(juris_label == "City of Vancouver")

sales_2015 <- van |>
  filter(year(sale_date) == 2015) |>
  select(folioid, act_use_cat, price_2015 = price)

sales_2025 <- van |>
  filter(year(sale_date) == 2025) |>
  select(folioid, price_2025 = price)
  • In deck 05, an average fell while every type rose, because the mix of homes sold changed. Comparing each home with its own earlier price avoids the mix.
  • folioid identifies a property. In bca, it starts in 2015.
  • select(price_2015 = price) keeps price under a new name. If both tables kept price, the join would return price.x and price.y.

Practice: how much did the same homes gain?

Join sales_2015 and sales_2025 so that only homes sold in both years remain.

  1. Before you join, check that folioid appears only once in each table.
  2. After you join, compute for each act_use_cat the number of homes and the median of price_2025 / price_2015 - 1.

Hint, if a folioid appears more than once: group_by(folioid) |> filter(n() == 1) |> ungroup() keeps the homes sold once that year.

Answer: check the keys

sales_2015 |> count(folioid) |> filter(n > 1) |> nrow()
[1] 262
sales_2025 |> count(folioid) |> filter(n > 1) |> nrow()
[1] 25

Some homes sold twice in one year. Joined as they are, a home sold twice in 2015 and twice in 2025 would become four rows. Keep the homes sold once in each year:

once_2015 <- sales_2015 |> group_by(folioid) |> filter(n() == 1) |> ungroup()
once_2025 <- sales_2025 |> group_by(folioid) |> filter(n() == 1) |> ungroup()
  • Most of the 2015 resales came a few months after the first sale: the median gap is 118 days.
  • Dropping them is a choice, and it should be stated. Keeping the last 2015 sale and the first 2025 sale is another.
  • relationship = "one-to-one" on the next slide would stop the join if any duplicate were left.

Answer: join and summarise

repeat_sales <- once_2015 |>
  inner_join(once_2025, by = "folioid", relationship = "one-to-one")
nrow(repeat_sales)
[1] 551
repeat_sales |>
  group_by(act_use_cat) |>
  summarise(homes = n(), median_growth = median(price_2025 / price_2015 - 1))
# A tibble: 2 × 3
  act_use_cat homes median_growth
  <chr>       <int>         <dbl>
1 S/F Res       107         0.505
2 Strata Res    444         0.592

Of 13,929 sales in 2015, 551 homes sold again, once, in 2025.

  • 15 of the 107 houses have a different year_built in 2025: a new house on the old lot. Their price change includes the construction.
  • One house “fell” 98%: its 2025 price is $17,420, which is not a market sale. The median is not affected.

Same homes, a different story
